Making the world’s quantitative visual knowledge machine-readable

01Introduction

Most of the world’s quantitative knowledge is drawn, not written. Reading it back accurately, at scale, is a layer the AI stack still lacks.

Scientific results live in plots. Market history lives in charts. Engineering limits live in datasheet curves. In every quantitative field, the chart is how numbers travel from the people who measured them to the people who decide with them.

The table behind a chart is often not published with it. In a broker report, a decades-old paper or a scanned filing, the picture may be the only surviving record of the data. Language models have made written knowledge searchable and computable, but the numbers inside these pictures remain largely out of reach.

This is not a minor gap. As AI is put to work accelerating science, informing policy and driving economic decisions, numbers it cannot read become a bottleneck.

Cambridge Visual Intelligence Labs (CVIL) is a research-first AI company developing models that recover and reason over quantitative information encoded in images. Our research combines geometric and semantic understanding, specialised model training and automated evaluation, aiming for models with high numerical fidelity that run on-premises at a fraction of the computational cost of general-purpose models.

Our first model, DeepChart, focuses on one of the clearest manifestations of this problem: recovering the numerical data behind charts. It provides a concrete, measurable test of our research approach, and the foundation for a broader programme in quantitative visual intelligence. It is also the first step towards AI that can learn from humanity’s accumulated experimental evidence and leverage it for model training and scientific discovery.

CVIL was founded by researchers from the University of Cambridge and combines expertise in visual computing, generative AI and production engineering. Its founding team includes researchers with experience at Cambridge, Google Research and Microsoft, alongside engineering leadership with a track record of building financial technology systems at scale.

02Quantitative knowledge is locked in images

The scale of quantitative information embedded in scientific figures is already enormous. In 2017, an analysis of just 650,000 articles from PubMed Central identified approximately 3.6 million individual plots, accounting for 35% of all classified figure panels (Lee, West and Howe, 2017). The archive has since grown to over 11 million articles. Yet access to the underlying numerical data remains limited. A systematic review published in the BMJ, encompassing 105 meta-research studies across 31 medical and health specialties, estimated that only 2% of articles published between 2016 and 2021 made their research data publicly available (Hamilton et al., 2023). Access to scientific publications, therefore, does not necessarily translate into access to their quantitative evidence.

The value of recovering this information is already being demonstrated. In materials science alone, the Starrydata project has reconstructed over 194,000 experimental property curves from more than 13,000 scientific publications, transforming published figures into datasets suitable for computational modelling and materials discovery (Katsura et al., 2025). The same problem extends far beyond academic research: financial reports, investment research, engineering datasheets and government publications all communicate quantitative information through charts. Even within a single financial institution, access to reports does not necessarily imply access to the underlying data. Organisational and technological silos can leave teams dependent on reports and dashboards produced by other departments, with obtaining the original data requiring multiple layers of coordination and approval (Grassi, Figini and Fedeli, 2022). Wherever the underlying data is unavailable, recovering the numbers from the image becomes a prerequisite for computational analysis. As AI systems increasingly undertake quantitative research, this limitation becomes a bottleneck to their ability to exploit existing knowledge.

Charts are Next

Each kind of information that machines learned to read at scale became a new layer of computing (Figure 1). Optical character recognition made printed pages searchable (Lee, 2019), speech recognition matched professional transcribers on a standard benchmark in 2016 (Xiong et al., 2016), document parsing turned layouts and tables into structure, tabular foundation models such as Prior Labs’ TabPFN learned to predict directly from tables (Hollmann et al., 2025), and language models made text something machines can reason over. Charts are next.

03From numbers to pixels and back again

Every chart is a table of numbers passed through a piece of software, a chart renderer. Reading a chart means running that software backwards.

A line chart in a central bank report, a dose–response curve in a clinical paper and a sensor trace in a datasheet were all made the same way: a table of values was passed to plotting software and rendered in a chosen style. The chart is the data, compressed through a renderer: scaled onto axes, drawn as marks, labelled with ticks and a legend.

Reading it back is an inverse rendering problem (Figure 2). Recover the axes and their scale. Separate each series from the gridlines, the labels and the other series. Map every pixel of every mark back to a value. Each step can add error, and one mistake in calibration moves every number on the chart.

Accuracy Matters

A chart becomes data only when it is read with pixel-level accuracy: every value as close to the truth as the image itself allows. Approximate reading is enough to describe a chart (the line rises, the peak comes in March) but not to compute with. A value that is a few percent off is simply a wrong number in a model, a spreadsheet or a filing, and nothing downstream will flag it.

Pixel-level accuracy is the natural target because the pixels are all the information a chart holds: no reader, human or machine, can recover more than they contain. Falling short of it is costly. On a chart of bond yields, a reading a few pixels off can be several basis points wrong, an error no bond analyst would accept.

04Our research approach

Geometry, learning and verifiable data

General-purpose vision-language models learn to interpret visual content across a broad range of tasks. Quantitative visual understanding, however, has a distinctive requirement: numerical outputs must remain faithful to the geometry of the original image. Our research explores how specialised models can combine visual-semantic understanding with geometric measurement, trained and evaluated on data whose numerical ground truth is known. This allows us to optimise for numerical fidelity, inference speed and computational efficiency at the same time.

Why specialised models matter

Frontier AI models are becoming increasingly capable of recovering numerical data from charts. Their progress shows that quantitative visual understanding is achievable, but it does not remove the case for specialised systems. General-purpose models are optimised for breadth. DeepChart is designed for a narrower objective: recovering numerical information with high fidelity, predictable latency and low computational cost. We believe that specialised models, trained on carefully constructed datasets and evaluated against exact numerical ground truth, can offer a substantially better efficiency frontier for quantitative visual tasks.

Extra thinking can buy accuracy, but not reliably, and always at a price in time and money. Raising reasoning effort has been shown to improve accuracy, while also leading to a disproportionate increase in compute spending (Berkane and Majumder, 2026). This is reflected in our own benchmarks (section 06).

Speed matters as much as cost. A reading that takes minutes cannot sit inside an analyst’s workflow, or inside an agent that calls tools many times to answer one question.

Frontier progress can strengthen a specialised model rather than replace it, through a specific mechanism. Frontier models can label what published charts mean (which series is which, what each axis measures), and a small model can learn from those labels together with charts whose data we know exactly. As the teachers improve, so do the labels the small model learns from, while it stays small, fast and private. Distillation of this kind is well established: a model 700 times smaller than PaLM outperformed it on a benchmark after training on PaLM’s step-by-step explanations (Hsieh et al., 2023), and ChartGemma, a 3-billion-parameter model trained on instructions that Gemini Flash wrote from real chart images, reached the state of the art on five chart benchmarks (Masry et al., 2025).

Data privacy is critical for financial institutions. Banks, insurers and companies with sensitive research often cannot send their documents to an external model at all, so whatever reads their charts must run on-premises, on hardware they control. On-premises deployment is therefore a design objective for DeepChart.

05DeepChart: our first model

DeepChart is our first implementation of this research direction. It sees only the chart image and returns the data points of each curve. Specialised image-processing components measure the chart’s geometry in pixels, and language models interpret what it means: its labels, legend and axis scales. Figure 3 shows two charts read by DeepChart.

Building on prior work

Chart extraction has a decade of research behind it, and DeepChart builds on three lines of it. The first pairs learned detectors with hand-written rules for each chart type, which later work replaced with learned components (Luo et al., 2021; Shivasankaran, Hassan and Singh, 2023; Cheng, Dai and Hauptmann, 2023). The second trains vision-language models to translate a chart straight into a table (Liu et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2023b; Masry et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), often learning from generated charts (Han et al., 2023) or with training aimed at charts that print no values (He et al., 2026). The third is distillation, in which a large model teaches a small one (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015). The small model is trained to reproduce the large model’s outputs rather than learning from labelled examples alone, which lets it keep much of the teacher’s ability on a narrow task at a fraction of the size and cost. Recent work distils explanations and instructions as well as answers, including for charts (Hsieh et al., 2023; Masry et al., 2025).

DeepChart combines all three: it measures geometry in pixels, it reads meaning with a language model, and it is designed to learn from larger models.

06Experimental results

We compare DeepChart with general-purpose models on accuracy, time and cost per chart together, because reading charts at scale needs all three (Figure 4).

DeepChart is the fastest and the cheapest configuration we tested (Figure 4). It averages 95% accuracy in 6 seconds for about $0.003 per chart. It sits beyond the frontier of general-purpose models on both time and cost (Figure 5): none is faster or cheaper, and the four that read more accurately take more than twice as long and cost at least thirty times as much.

The most accurate models now read charts well, averaging up to about 96% on this test, but at very different prices. Astra 6.2 at max reasoning effort is the most accurate, at more than five minutes and $0.79 per chart; Fable 5.2 at low effort is almost as accurate in 13 seconds, for $0.09. More reasoning does not reliably buy accuracy: for Astra, Fable and Opus, the highest effort took 10 to 53 times longer per chart than the lowest, and Fable was less accurate on average at its highest effort than at its lowest.

On accuracy DeepChart is within one point of the best: four general-purpose configurations are still ahead, the most accurate at 95.7% against 95.1%. Closing that gap without giving up speed or cost is our main technical focus.

Scale makes the cost difference concrete. At these averages, a million charts would cost about $790,000 with Astra 6.2 at max effort, $90,000 with Fable 5.2 at low effort, and about $3,000 with DeepChart.

How we measure. We ran 11 general-purpose models at three levels of reasoning effort each, 33 configurations in all. Each model sees only the chart image and returns the data behind it. In Figures 4 and 5, accuracy is the ChartInfo metric for continuous data, averaged over 190 charts drawn by our generator from known data. Time is the mean per chart. Cost is the mean per chart in US dollars, estimated for DeepChart.

07Research engine and Benchmark

Our evaluation environment generates charts whose data we know exactly, has our method and frontier models read them, scores how closely each reading matches the true data, and shows every result for inspection.

Variety matters more than volume: a model trained on charts that all look alike fails on the first chart drawn in another style. Our data generator varies a large number of chart parameters including ticks, grids, legends, markers, palettes, axis scales and the number of series, and includes hard cases such as overlapping lines, high-frequency signals and uncertainty bands.

Every result can be searched and filtered by chart style, number of series or score, and each reading can be opened with its curves drawn over the true ones. Failures can be found, not only counted.

The development environment automatises the process of model evaluation, closing a rapid feedback loop that accelerates model improvement. A coding agent can change our method, run the benchmark, study the scores and the charts it got wrong, and try again.

This loop turns the benchmark into a research engine. Every change to our method is scored on charts with known answers, allowing us to measure progress version by version, identify systematic weaknesses and optimise accuracy, latency and computational cost simultaneously. As we extend our models to new chart types and understanding tasks, this environment provides a common framework for measuring and accelerating progress.

08From chart intelligence to scientific discovery

Chart digitisation addresses an immediate and commercially important problem. But it also raises a broader question: what becomes possible when machines can reliably access the world’s quantitative visual knowledge? We see three research stages (Figure 8), extending from universal chart understanding to autonomous scientific discovery. Each addresses a distinct problem, with the first providing a technological foundation for the others.

Stage 1Universal chart intelligence

Our immediate ambition is to become the default AI layer for chart understanding: the technology that software systems and AI agents rely on whenever they encounter quantitative information encoded in a chart.

Just as optical character recognition made printed text accessible to software, we aim to make charts directly accessible to computational systems. This requires more than numerical extraction. A machine must understand what the numbers represent, recognise variables and units, reconstruct relationships, answer quantitative questions and check that its answers are supported by the chart.

Achieving this across chart types, visual styles and scientific domains, with high numerical fidelity, low computational cost and predictable latency, is itself a substantial research challenge.

DeepChart is our first model towards this objective. We envisage its capabilities becoming embedded in document processing systems, research platforms, enterprise software and the broader ecosystem of AI agents.

Stage 2Training data for AI models

A chart reader serves AI models in two ways. As a tool, it is called whenever a model or agent meets a chart; that is the first stage. As a source of training data, it turns the numbers held in charts into something models can learn from; that is the second. Training data means reading charts by the million, so the cost of reading each one decides what is feasible (section 06).

Language models may soon run out of public text to learn from. One study expects them to have used the whole public stock between 2026 and 2032 (Villalobos et al., 2024), and an international report by 100 AI experts finds demand growing fast enough to exhaust human-generated internet text by 2030 (Bengio et al., 2025). In 2024, OpenAI co-founder Ilya Sutskever put it plainly: “the data is not growing, because we have but one internet” (Dastin, 2024). The values in published charts are measured data, and very little of it has been extracted.

The same applies to organisations that adapt models to their own documents: years of reports and presentations whose charts were drawn for people to read, and whose source spreadsheets may no longer exist. Extracting those values lets a model learn from the numbers directly.

Long-term visionAutonomous scientific discovery

Much of humanity’s experimental knowledge is fragmented across millions of scientific publications, which often communicate their results through charts rather than releasing the underlying data. Reliable chart understanding creates an opportunity to reconstruct this evidence at scale, with each measurement tied to its experimental conditions, units and provenance.

An AI system able to access and integrate this evidence could go beyond retrieving existing conclusions and conduct original investigations: finding relationships that emerge only across many studies, identifying contradictions between published experiments, testing hypotheses against existing observations and proposing new experiments. Our long-term research ambition is to develop the technologies that enable such systems, grounded not only in the written descriptions of scientific knowledge but also in the numerical evidence from which that knowledge emerges.

09From research to deployment

Financial services is our first proving ground. It demands numerical accuracy, its documents are confidential, and its use cases matter to daily operations. Investment analysts read research full of charts: forecasts, peer comparisons, price and yield histories. The numbers in those charts rarely reach a spreadsheet unless someone retypes them, so most are read once by eye and never used again.

Applied AI teams at banks are building agents that read documents. Those agents read the text and miss the charts, or must send each page to an external model that compliance may not allow. An accurate, private chart reader is a missing component in that stack. Finance also sets the standard we want to meet everywhere: values right to the basis point and deployment inside the institution. A system that meets that bar can serve science, engineering and public data next.

We plan two forms of deployment. Private deployment or licensing lets an institution run DeepChart on its own hardware; it is what finance needs, and it depends on the smaller model now in development. API access lets developers and AI agents call DeepChart and pay for each chart they read, through MCP registries and model marketplaces; it needs no local deployment, and becomes more compelling as DeepChart gets faster.

Beyond direct institutional deployment, we see an opportunity for chart intelligence to become a standard component of the AI infrastructure stack. Document intelligence platforms, research agents and enterprise AI systems increasingly need to operate across heterogeneous information sources. Rather than developing their own specialised chart-reading capabilities, these systems could delegate quantitative visual understanding to CVIL’s models. Our objective is to make that integration more accurate, efficient and economical than relying on general-purpose models alone.

10Conclusion

Charts are how the world shares numbers, and AI cannot yet read them back at the accuracy, speed and cost that real work needs. Our thesis is that specialised models, grounded in geometry and trained and tested against exact numerical ground truth, can read them with high fidelity at a fraction of the cost of general-purpose models. DeepChart is the first test of that thesis, and our results show where it stands today.

We believe that chart intelligence will become an essential layer of the AI stack. CVIL is building the specialised models that will allow machines to access quantitative visual information accurately, efficiently and at scale.

But reading this information is only the beginning. Humanity has accumulated an extraordinary body of experimental evidence, much of it fragmented across scientific publications and encoded in figures. Making that evidence computationally accessible could open new possibilities for scientific discovery: uncovering relationships, resolving contradictions and identifying experiments that have yet to be performed.

Our ambition is to build towards that future, beginning with a fundamental capability that AI still lacks: the ability to reliably read what the world has drawn, not only what it has written.
